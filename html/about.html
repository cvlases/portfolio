// how I got here

The most challenging project I have ever worked on was my very first coding project, a simple text-based story game in Python.

Since then, my work has grown much more complex. I have published peer-reviewed research applying machine learning to cancer datasets, authored internal AI policy for the state of Montana, and I am currently at Carnegie Mellon University designing spatial user interfaces for on-body computing.

But the hardest project was, without question, learning how to begin.

I did not grow up around technology. I was raised on a small farm in Montana, where we kept sheep and chickens, sold produce to local restaurants, and learned to fix things with whatever resources were on hand. I did not have a smartphone, a computer, or video games. When I got to college and tried to navigate the online portal to register for classes, I accidentally enrolled in a computer science course. I could not figure out how to unregister, so I just showed up to the lecture.

The learning curve was steep and painstaking. Using the terminal felt entirely daunting, and my classmates possessed a fluency with logic and syntax that made me feel like I was watching people speak a foreign language. In those early weeks, I did not even have the mental vocabulary to name what I was confused about.

What got me through was not a sudden breakthrough, but a deliberate decision to stop trying to look smart and focus instead on staying curious. I spent every spare hour in office hours. Slowly, the terminal error messages stopped feeling like the computer telling me I was wrong and started feeling like useful feedback.

When my little interactive game finally ran without crashing, I sat with that feeling for a long time. It was not sophisticated code by any measure, but I had built an environment that responded directly to a human being, and that felt extraordinary.

I was completely hooked. I took extra math courses over the summers, graduated with my computer science degree, and earned a full scholarship to pursue my master’s degree in Human-Computer Interaction at Carnegie Mellon.

Growing up in Montana taught me to pay close attention to the world around me, to value resourcefulness, and to learn by doing. It is exactly my motivation every day: creating tools that serve real people.
